The character 學 (xué), "to learn", hides two hands. Today, no one sees them anymore.

In modern Chinese, it takes eight strokes. It once took sixteen. Between the two: three thousand years, an emperor, a dictionary… and two hands fading away. To find them, we go back to the diviners of the Shang, who carved the answers of the ancestors into turtle shells. At the heart of the sign, crossed sticks for counting; below, a roof; on either side, two hands reaching for the sticks.

Under the Zhou, a child slips in beneath the roof, and the sign says it all: hands passing on, under a roof, to a child. Then the First Emperor imposes a single script, the small seal of his minister Li Si. Around the year 100, the scholar Xu Shen gives the sign its definition in the Shuowen Jiezi: to learn is to awaken.

But the hands were not erased by decree. They were erased by other hands: those of hurried scribes, who flattened the curves and shrank the whole top of the character to three little strokes, the form Japan and then China adopted in the twentieth century. The hands have faded from the sign. Not from the gesture: for three thousand years, to learn has meant to receive what other hands pass on.

Language notes

学 / 學 (xué) — to learn, to study
Compound ideogram: sticks (爻) + two hands (臼) + roof (冖) + child (子) — HSK 1
Simplified 学 (8 strokes), traditional 學 (16 strokes)

学习 (xué xí): to learn / to study.
学生 (xué sheng): student / pupil.
学校 (xué xiào): school.
大学 (dà xué): university.
同学 (tóng xué): classmate.
